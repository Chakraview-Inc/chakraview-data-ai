Five Versions of the Truth

Why revenue, customer count and churn come out different depending on who you ask, why another dashboard won't settle it, and what a company with one answer has that you don't.

By Narendra Loganathan • 7 min read • Updated 2026-11-06

It is the quarterly review. Sales reports 4,812 active customers. Finance reports 4,390. The support dashboard says 5,207. The next twenty minutes go to arguing about whose number is right, and the meeting ends with an action item to "reconcile the numbers" that everyone in the room knows will be back next quarter.

Here is the uncomfortable part: nobody is wrong. Each of those numbers is correct by the rules of the department that produced it. There are three numbers in this meeting, and in most companies two more turn up as soon as someone opens marketing's spreadsheet or last quarter's board deck. The company has five versions of the truth, and no agreed way to choose between them.

You are not the only one. When the finance software vendor OneStream surveyed more than 350 senior finance and IT executives in May 2026, 61% said they second-guess their data at least monthly, only about half had a consistent source of truth or automated reconciliation across their systems, and 47% had made a material business decision on inaccurate or outdated data in the past year. Customer records are a big part of it. Data quality vendor Validity's survey of CRM administrators found duplicate records among the most common problems, and its report cites Salesforce research putting duplicates at more than a quarter of the average contact database.

Three ways the truth splits

1. Definitions

To sales, an active customer is anyone who bought something in the last 90 days. To finance, it is anyone with a paid invoice this quarter. To support, it is anyone who opened a ticket this year. "Revenue" has the same problem: it can mean booked, billed or recognized, and each is the right answer to a different question. Every one of these definitions is reasonable. What is missing is a written decision about which one the company means when it says the word.

2. Identity

The same customer usually exists several times over. It is "Acme Corp" in the CRM, "ACME Corporation" in billing, and a contact's email address in the support tool. Count customers in each system and you are counting different things. The obvious fix, merging records that look alike, has a nasty failure of its own: merge carelessly and you combine two different customers into one, and now one of them is getting the other's invoices.

We have seen how quietly this goes wrong in one of our own reference platforms. The shortcut it used to give each customer an ID was too short. Past roughly 77,000 customers, the odds that two different people shared an ID passed fifty-fifty. To prove it, we generated test customers until two of them collided. Nothing raised an error. Two people simply became one. No single report would ever have shown it.

3. Timing

Finance closes the month on the fifth working day. The CRM changes by the minute. Support's export ran last Tuesday. Even with one definition and one identity, three numbers taken at three different moments will not match, and nobody can tell from the slide which moment each one came from.

There is often a fourth culprit: the spreadsheet in the middle, where someone adjusts a figure by hand on its way to the board deck, for reasons that made sense at the time. That one deserves a guide of its own.

Why it matters more now

For years, conflicting numbers were an irritation rather than a danger, because a person stood between the data and the decision. An experienced analyst knew that the board deck uses finance's count, that support's number includes people who never bought anything, and that the CRM counts a customer twice if their contact changed companies. None of that judgment was written down, but it was applied every time someone asked.

Put an AI assistant in front of the same data and that judgment is not there. Ask the AI how many active customers you have, and it will pick one definition, silently, and answer with complete confidence. It has no way of knowing which rule your people would have applied. The disagreement does not go away. It just stops being visible, and a decision gets made on whichever version the AI happened to find first.

Plain-language glossary

Single source of truth. One agreed place, and one agreed definition, for each number the business steers by.

Customer identity. The way every system recognizes that a record refers to the same real customer.

Duplicate record. One real customer stored as two or more records, often with small differences in spelling.

Reconciliation. Checking that two systems agree, and explaining the difference when they do not.

Why the obvious fixes don't stick

A new dashboard. It shows the disagreement more attractively, or quietly picks a winner and hides the others. Either way the argument resumes the first time someone pulls the number from a different system.

A data warehouse. Bringing everything into one place is genuinely useful. But a warehouse holds all five versions of the truth side by side. It does not decide between them.

A data dictionary. Written with good intentions, reviewed once, and never updated. Within months it describes a company that no longer exists, and people stop opening it.

A central team that owns every definition. This one sounds like the grown-up answer, and it quietly becomes the bottleneck. The people who run a process understand what its numbers mean. A central team ends up guessing on their behalf, and every new question waits in its queue.

What good looks like

A company with one version of the truth is not one with a single giant system. It is one where a handful of things are true:

  • Every core metric has one written definition and a named business owner, and the owner is the person who runs that part of the business, not IT.
  • A small shared vocabulary covers what crosses departments, such as customer, order and revenue, while each department keeps ownership of its own detail.
  • Each customer has one identity, assigned in one place and honored by every system that refers to them.
  • Disagreements are caught automatically when data arrives, not discovered in the meeting.
  • Every reported number can be traced to its definition, its sources and the moment it was taken.

The same question, three honest answers. One version of the truth comes from decisions about definition, ownership and identity, not from a better chart. Figures are illustrative.

Where it gets hard

Each of those properties is simple to state. The work is in the decisions behind them.

Which numbers first. Trying to settle every metric at once is how these efforts die. The skill is picking the few numbers the business actually steers by, and getting those right before touching the rest.

Who decides. When sales and finance disagree about what "active" means, someone has to make the call, and the call has to stick. That is a question of governance and, frankly, politics, more than technology. Getting it wrong produces a definition that everyone politely ignores.

How strict to be about identity. Match customers too loosely and you merge different people. Match too strictly and the duplicates stay. The right balance is different for finance, for marketing and for anything touching privacy, and a single setting for all three is usually wrong for at least one of them.

What happens to history. When a definition changes, last year's numbers change with it, or they don't. Restating the past is honest and confusing. Not restating it makes trend lines meaningless. Either way, someone has to explain to the board why the line moved.

Keeping it light. The whole effort fails if it becomes a new queue that every report has to wait in. The structure has to keep ownership with the departments while keeping the shared parts consistent, and that balance is easy to lose in either direction.

These are the calls where experience saves the most time, because the wrong call does not fail loudly. It just produces a sixth version of the truth.

A self-check

  1. Ask three departments for last month's active customer count. Do you get one number?
  2. Who decides what "revenue" means in your company, and where is that decision written down?
  3. If the same customer is in your CRM and your billing system, how does each system know it is the same customer?
  4. When a number in the board deck is questioned, how long does it take to trace it to its source?
  5. The last time a definition changed, did past reports change with it, or only future ones?
  6. If an AI assistant answers "how many customers do we have?", which definition does it use?

If the honest answer to the first question is "no," the rest tell you where the versions are coming from.

One answer is a decision, not a tool

One version of the truth is not something you buy. It is a small set of decisions about meaning, identity and ownership that, once made and enforced, stop the same argument from happening every quarter. It also has a payoff that is easy to miss: every report, every dashboard and every AI system you add afterward inherits those decisions instead of inventing its own.
